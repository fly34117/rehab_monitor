A B S T R A C T   
Background: Gait changes with aging have been investigated, but few studies have examined a wide range of gait 
parameters across the adult lifespan. This study aimed to investigate gait differences across age groups stratified 
by sex. 
Methods: This cross-sectional study included 629 healthy, normal-weight (i.e., BMI < 30 kg/m2) participants 
from Switzerland of the COmPLETE cohort study, aged 20 to over 90 years. Gait metrics were assessed using an 
inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based gait analysis system, including speed, cycle duration variability, asym­
metry, stride length, cycle duration, cadence, double support, stance (time foot is on the ground during a gait 
cycle), swing (time foot is in the air during a gait cycle), loading (early part of the stance phase), foot-flat (mid- 
stance phase when foot is flat), and pushing (late stance phase leading to toe-off) phases. Percentile curves were 
calculated using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape. 
Results: Gait data from 545 participants (273 men and 272 women) were analyzed. Participants were equally 
distributed across the seven age decades, with an average of 40 men and 40 women representing every decade. 
Both men and women showed a reduction in gait speed and stride length, and an increase in cycle duration 
variability and asymmetry with aging. Gait speed and stride length declined across the age groups, with a sig­
nificant difference found in participants aged 80 to 91 compared to younger age groups. 
Significance: Age-related changes in gait parameters were seen in both men and women. These may be attributed 
to the typical decline in muscle strength, balance, coordination, and neuromuscular function. The results of this 
study contribute to the understanding of gait changes throughout the lifespan and can be used for comparison 
with other populations and as reference values for individual patients.   
1. Introduction 
Walking is a complex task learned during childhood and performed 
almost automatically throughout life. Degenerative changes to the 
muscles and neuromuscular system related to aging can alter gait pat­
terns in older adults, leading to decreased gait velocity, stride length, 
joint movement, and an increased gait stiffness [1]. These changes are 
associated with an increased risk of falls and fear of falling [1,2], which 
further affect gait quality [3]. 
Subtle changes in gait patterns may occur over the lifespan, detect­
able only through sensitive assessment tools like pressure-sensitive 
walkways, camera-based or inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based

gait analysis systems. Reference values for gait parameters across the 
lifespan help to evaluate an individual’s walking ability, to assess the 
impact of gait on health outcomes, to screen for intrinsic risk factors for 
falls or disease indicators, and to evaluate clinical intervention effects. 
A 2018 systematic review revealed a lack of data on spatiotemporal 
gait parameters across the entire lifespan, in a healthy population with 
most studies focusing on either older or younger adults [4]. The authors 
concluded that data concerning middle-aged adults are lacking (age of 
30 up to the age of 59) to understand the effects of aging on gait. The 
only existing study covering a wide age range used the GAITrite 
walkway system in a healthy Asian population [5]. It has been shown 
that gait parameters differ between ehnic/racial groups [6,7]. To the 
best of our knowledge, there are no data available from Europe covering 
the entire adult lifespan. Comprehensive data are needed for a better 
understanding of when and how gait changes occur. 
Sensor-based systems like the GaitUp Physilog®5 have become 
widely used in gait analysis due to their flexibility and capability to 
assess longer distances than traditional systems like GAITRite®. A recent 
study showed substantial agreement between the two systems regarding 
key gait parameters [8]. For our study, we chose the sensor-based 
Physilog®5 system for its feasibility, alignment with current gait anal­
ysis trends, and the ability to assess longer walking distances compared 
to a carpet-based system. 
There is a relatively large body of literature focusing on gait in 
people with specific conditions [9–11]. This is likely because under­
standing gait changes in people with conditions like Parkinson’s disease, 
stroke, multiple sclerosis, or osteoarthritis can provide valuable insights 
into the disease progression, and help identify potential interventions. 
However, studying gait in healthy individuals is also important, as it 
helps to establish normative data and reference values for various gait 
parameters. This can be useful for comparing gait patterns in individuals 
with and without specific conditions and for identifying early warning 
signs of potential health issues. Therefore, our study aimed to close this 
gap by providing an overview of spatiotemporal gait parameters in 
healthy Swiss adult individuals using a wearable sensor system placed 
on the shoes. The specific aims were: a) to investigate gait differences 
across age groups stratified by sex; and b) to illustrate age-related 
changes by percentile curves. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Study population and recruitment 
We recruited healthy participants from the Basel area, Switzerland, 
within the COmPLETE cohort study comprising approximately 40 males 
and 40 females per age category (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 
70–79, and 80 + years). General invitation letters with information on 
the study aims and procedures were mailed to randomly selected dis­
tricts in the Basel area. The sample size justification, recruitment scheme 
and measurements are detailed in the published study protocol [12]. 
Interested individuals underwent a telephone screening before sched­
uling tests. Participants’ final eligibility was confirmed onsite on the day 
of examination. Prior to participation, participants provided written 
informed consent. Eligible individuals were healthy men and women 
aged 20 to 100 years with a body mass index of less than 30 kg/m2. 
Exclusion criteria were any manifest exercise-limiting chronic diseases, 
such as, among others, stroke, lower-extremity artery disease, orthope­
dic issues, or any form of dementia. 
The study was conducted at the Department of Sport, Exercise, and 
Health at the University of Basel, Switzerland. The Ethics Committee of 
Northwestern and Central Switzerland (EKNZ 2017–01451) approved 
the study. 
2.2. Procedures 
All measurements were conducted by trained assessors. We 
instructed the participants to walk at their usual walking speed and 
specifically pointed out that they do not need to walk at maximum 
speed. The instruction was as follows: “Please walk at your normal pace 
for 20 m to the pylon, go around the pylon, and then walk back the 20 m 
past the line.” 
The tests took place indoors in a corridor with a smooth and even 
surface. Height and body mass were measured to the nearest 0.5 cm and 
0.1 kg, respectively. 
2.3. Gait Parameters 
Gait parameters were measured using an IMU-based gait analysis 
system (Physilog®, Mindmaze, Lausanne, Switzerland), which allows 
motion analysis based on two lightweight inertial sensors, attached to 
the dorsum of both feet, at the height of the metatarsal bones. The 
system has high validity and reliability [8,13,14]. An overview of 
measured parameters and their definitions is given in Table 1. 
2.4. Statistical analysis 
Participant characteristics are presented as either means and stan­
dard deviations or median and interquartile ranges. 
In order to evaluate gait differences across age groups stratified by 
sex, we compared gait parameters between age categories (per decade) 
for men and women separately using a one-way analysis of variance test 
(ANOVA). In case of a significant effect of age category, we subsequently 
performed Tukey post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni 
correction of the p-values for multiple testing. All analyses were con­
ducted in R version 4.3.1 with an alpha level of significance set at 
p < 0.05. 
In order to graphically illustrate age-related changes, we provide sex- 
specific percentile curves generated with generalized additive models 
(GAMLSS, R package version 5.1–6). The age-trajectories were modeled 
using P-splines. The Bayesian information criterion selected the condi­
tional distribution that balanced model complexity and goodness-of-fit. 
Model fits were inspected using diagnostic residual plots (worm plots 
and Q-Q plots).

3. Results 
A total of 629 participants performed the walking test. For the 
analysis, valid gait data from 545 participants (273 men and 272 
women) were analyzed (see flow chart, Appendix 1). Participants were 
equally distributed across age groups from 20 to older than 80 years, 
with a minimum of 21 men and 30 women representing each decade. 
Participant baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2 by age 
group (i.e., per decade). 
3.1. Gait parameters across the adult age range 
The mean stride count per participant was 25.0 SD 2.9 (min 17, max 
38). Gait parameters for each age group are presented in Table 3, 
separated by sex. Standardized mean differences are presented in  
Table 4. The results of the post-hoc pairwise comparisons of the ANOVA 
analysis for men and women (when applicable) are presented in Ap­
pendix 2. 
The pattern of the different gait parameters over time (i.e. smooth­
ened percentile curves) for men and women are visualized in Figs. 1 and 
2, as well as supplementary Figures in the Appendix 3 and 4, providing a 
graphical representation of the changes across the lifespan. 
3.1.1. Men 
In men, we saw a significant effect of age decades for speed, stride 
length, cadence, cycle duration, the loading phase, and the foot-flat 
phase, with p-values ranging between 0.007 and < 0.001 (see 
Table 4). Looking at the post-hoc pairwise comparisons, we saw a sig­
nificant lower gait speed in men aged 80 and above, compared to men 
between 30 and 39 (p = 0.04), between 40 and 49 (p = 0.01), between 
50 and 59 (p = 0.019) and between 60 and 69 (p = 0.006). Cadence was 
significantly lower in men between 20 and 29 years old compared to 
men between 60 and 69 (p = 0.004) and between 70 and 79 
(p < 0.001). Stride length was significantly lower in older men. We 
found significant differences between men aged 80 and above compared 
to men in all age categories under 70 years (p < 0.001 for all compari­
sons). Men aged 70 to 79 years had a significantly lower stride length 
than men in all younger age categories (p < 0.02). Cycle duration was 
significantly higher in men aged 20 to 29 and men between 60 and 69, 
and 70 and 79 (p = 0.004 and p = 0.001, respectively). The loading 
phase was significantly lower in men aged 80 and above compared to 
men between 40 and 49 and 60 and 69 (p = 0.007 and p = 0.039, 
respectively). Finally, the foot-flat phase was significantly higher in men 
aged 80 and above compared to men between 30 and 39 (p = 0.009), 
between 40 and 49 (p = 0.004), and between 60 and 69 (p = 0.018). 
The results of pairwise comparisions are presented in Appendix 4. 
3.1.2. Women 
In women, we saw a significant effect of age decade on speed, stride 
length, cadence, cycle duration, the loading phase, the foot-flat phase, 
the cycle duration variability, and asymmetry with p-values ranging 
between 0.034 and < 0.001 (see Table 4). The post-hoc pairwise com­
parisons showed a significantly lower gait speed in women aged 80 and 
above compared to women below the age of 70 (p-values < 0.003). 
Furthermore, women between 70 and 79 years of age had a significantly 
lower gait speed than 50 to 59 year olds (p = 0.043). Stride length was 
significantly lower in women aged 80 and above compared to all 
younger age categories (p < 0.007), and in 70 to 79 year olds compared 
to women below 60 years of age (p < 0.001). Women between 60 and 69 
had a lower stride length than women between 30 and 59 years of age 
(p < 0.036). The loading phase was significantly lower and the foot-flat 
phase was significantly higher in women aged 80 and above compared 
to all younger age categories (p < 0.017 and p < 0.030, respectively). 
The cycle duration variability was higher in women aged 80 and above 
compared to all age categories until 59 years (p < 0.008) and asym­
metry was higher in the oldest age category compared to women until 49 
years of age (p < 0.019). The results of pairwise comparisions are pre­
sented in Appendix 2. 
4. Discussion 
4.1. Main findings 
In this study, we aimed to provide an overview of spatiotemporal gait 
parameters in healthy Swiss individuals aged 20 to 91 years, using an 
IMU-based gait analysis system. Our findings contribute to the under­
standing of gait patterns over the lifespan and may help identify risk 
factors for falls or indicators of disease. 
We found that gait speed, stride length, cycle duration, cadence, 
loading, and foot-flat phase were significantly different across age cat­
egories for both men and women. In general, gait speed and stride length 
decreased with age, while cadence increased. The proportion of the 
stance phase and double support phase also evolved similarly across age 
groups. 
Our results showed that gait patterns are relatively stable during 
early adulthood, with more pronounced changes occurring after the age 
of 60. Gait speed and stride length were found to be lower in older in­
dividuals. It is important to emphasize that the average gait speed for all

4.2. Interpretation 
Our observations suggest a compensatory increase in cadence among 
older age groups, possibly as an adaptive mechanism to maintain 
walking speed despite reductions in stride length. The observed changes 
in gait parameters with aging can be attributed to a range of interrelated 
factors. Age-related declines in muscle mass and strength, balance, and 
coordination [16–19], as well as alterations in the central and peripheral 
nervous systems [20,21], contribute to slower, more variable, and 
asymmetrical gait patterns. Additionally, a decline in motor unit number 
and function [22,23] exacerbates these effects. Joint health issues, such 
as osteoarthritis, limit movement and cause pain, leading to reduced gait 
speed and stride length [24]. Sensory declines, particularly in vision and 
proprioception, impact balance and spatial awareness, promoting more 
cautious gait patterns [25–27]. Cognitive factors, including multitasking 
abilities and attention, are also crucial, especially in older individuals, 
influencing gait dynamics [28]. Moreover, lifestyle choices and physical 
activity levels significantly influence gait performance as one ages [29, 
30]. These factors collectively affect stability and walking efficiency, 
often resulting in slower and less stable gait, characterized by increased 
cycle duration variability and asymmetry [31,32]. This variability is 
associated with a higher fall risk in older adults [1,33]. 
4.3. Comparison with other studies 
Most studies have focused on either older or younger healthy adults. 
To the best of our knowledge, the Yishun Study by Lau et al. (2020) is the 
only other study reporting gait parameters over the entire lifespan [5]. 
Their study focused on a Southeast Asian population (n = 507, from 
Singapore), whereas our study provides an overview for Swiss in­
dividuals from mostly Caucasian origin. It has been described that gait 
varies between demographic groups [6,7]. Specifically, a study by 
Boulifard et al. (2019) found significant variations in gait across 
different demographics with African American and Hispanic partici­
pants having lower gait speeds than their white counterparts. Consistent 
with our findings, they reported that older participants had slower 
speeds than younger ones. Unlike our study, they observed generally

et al. (2022) highlights that racial differences in gait speeds among fe­

Both the study by Lau et al. (2020) and our study focused on “healthy 
individuals” (i.e. several diseases listed as exclusion criteria). We used a 
wearable sensor system to measure gait parameters, which allowed for 
unobtrusive data collection in a natural walking environment. The 
Yishun Study employed the GAITRite electronic walkway system, a 
widely used tool in gait analysis (often considered as “the golden stan­
dard”). This system requires participants to walk on a special mat with 
integrated sensors to record gait parameters. Although both methods are 
valid for gait analysis, our approach allowed for a more naturalistic 
assessment and longer walking distances (i.e. two times 20 m per 
attempt) [5]. 
Interestingly, for both females and males, participants in our study 
consistently showed higher gait speeds, longer step/stride lengths, and 
increased cadence across all age groups, compared to the Asian study. 
Both studies involved healthy participants who were instructed to walk 
at their habitual pace. A notable distinction is that participants in our 
study wore shoes, while those in the Yishun Study walked barefoot [5]. 
We are not aware of any studies explicitly linking these observed dif­
ferences to whether participants are barefoot or shod. Therefore, the 
variations in gait characteristics observed in our study may be attrib­
utable to ethnic differences and/or disparities in test conditions. 
In our study, we found significant differences in gait speed, stride 
length, cycle duration, cadence, loading, and foot-flat phase across age 
categories for both men and women. Gait speed and stride length 
generally decreased with age, while cadence increased. These changes 
were more pronounced in older individuals, particularly after the age of 
60. The Yishun Study also reported age-related declines in gait speed 
and stride length, as well as increases in stance time and double support 
time, which are consistent with our findings [5]. Unlike our study, the 
Yishun Study did not report an increase in cadence with age. This dif­
ference might be attributed to the different populations studied, an 
Asian urban population versus a Swiss suburban and rural population. 
The differences observed between the two studies highlight the impor­
tance of considering population-specific factors when interpreting gait 
parameters and their potential clinical implications. 
There are further studies reporting a decline in gait speed and stride 
length with aging [31,34]. The observed changes in cycle duration 
variability and asymmetry in our study are in line with previous research 
[4], which reported an increase in cycle duration variability with age 
and the association between age and gait asymmetry [35]. The results of 
our study contribute to the understanding of gait changes throughout 
the lifespan and can be compared to other populations to assess the 
generalizability of our findings. 
4.4. Strengths and limitations 
Strengths of this study include the large sample size and the wide age 
range of participants, which allowed for the investigation of gait 
changes at all ages between 20 and 91 years. Furthermore, the use of a 
reliable and validated system for gait assessment (Physilog 5) ensured 
accurate and consistent data collection [13,14]. 
The primary limitation of this study is that only one trial was 
captured per participant. However, the analysis was based on an average 
of 25 strides per participant, which is sufficient according to the liter­
ature [36]. 
Our study is founded on robust data derived from Swiss individuals, 
predominantly of Caucasian origin. While inclusive and diverse research 
is preferred, such inclusivity was not feasible in our context due to 
challenges in recruiting a balanced number of participants from various 
ethnic groups. Nonetheless, focusing primarily on one ethnic group, as 
our study does, can yield detailed and specific insights pertinent to the 
group in question. 
5. Conclusion 
Our study provides valuable insights into the changes in gait pa­
rameters from 20 to over 90 years of age, revealing a decline in gait 
speed and stride length, and an increase in cadence, cycle duration 
variability, and asymmetry with aging. These findings provide valuable 
reference values for clinical practice and can inform interventions aimed 
at maintaining or improving gait performance in older adults. Targeted 
exercise programs focusing on strength, balance, and coordination could 
be particularly effective in preserving normal gait characteristics and 
reducing fall risk.
